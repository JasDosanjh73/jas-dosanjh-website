‘Jas, this is Af. She wanted to come over and say hello,’ said Roshna.

I glanced up from where I was sitting with Maria. I already knew Af by sight. She looked like a lanky giraffe and as if she wasn’t tall enough already, she wore heels. Her long, black hair swung loosely around her hips, her clothes always clung tightly to her curves, her cheeks were brushed with pink blush and she wore bright red lipstick smeared across her lips which always seemed to hang open in a wide grin. Even now she was standing next to Roshna with a big smile plastered across her face.

‘Hi,’ I said and I turned back around, not bothering to hear her reply.

Af and her two older sisters were not like any of the other Muslim girls at Alumwell. Openly flirting and throwing coy glances at all the boys, they wore full faces of make-up and could be found constantly preening themselves in front of the mirrors in the girls’ toilets before they’d pout and strut down the corridors.

Af was everything that I’d been raised not to be: vain and flirty, she walked with a swagger of her hips and she was always grinning as if she didn’t have a care in the world. Somehow, she’d become an acquaintance of Roshna’s.

The following morning, as I headed out of my English class, I heard someone call my name. Looking up, I saw Af.

‘Hi!’ She flashed her wide grin at me.

‘Oh… hi.’ Taken aback by her presence outside my classroom, I carried on walking down the corridor. Much to my annoyance, Af fell into step beside me.

Her next words, stated as a matter of fact, shocked me. ‘You think you’re too good for me, don’t you?’

Mortified that she could be so frank and flabbergasted that she’d read me so well, I stumbled around for an answer. ‘Oh… no… no… I don’t… I don’t.’ I expected Af to look hurt or be angry, but instead she tossed her head back and laughed out loud.

She grinned. ‘You don’t lie very well, do you?’

I fell into an embarrassed silence. I’d assumed Af was stupid because she only cared about how she looked and how many boys she could attract, but somehow, she’d managed to read me perfectly. Clearly, she wasn’t a complete airhead.

As we continued down the corridor, I noticed that every boy who walked past us had stared open-mouthed at her with their eyes wide as saucers. Af was swinging her hips and grinning like the cat who’d got the cream. Part of me felt so uncomfortable with all the attention she was generating that I wanted to get away from her as quickly as I could, but a part of me also wanted to put her in her place. I turned to her.

‘Can’t you even walk down the corridor without putting on a fucking show?’

To my amazement, Af exploded with laughter. ‘Oh my God… you’re so funny, Jas!’

With growing infuriation, I begrudgingly plodded along, wishing that either Af would magically disappear or that I could. She chuckled softly all the way to the doors which led to the playground, but in the throng of other children, I managed to lose her.

A few days later, as I chatted with my friends at dinnertime, Af walked across to us. With a big smile, she greeted everyone, but her eyes came to rest on me.

‘Hi Jas.’

Not inclined to be cruel, I gave her a short smile.

‘Does anyone wanna come to the shops with me?’ she asked.

I opened my mouth to tell her I didn’t, but to my surprise my friends readily agreed to go with her. Not wanting to be left behind, I trailed after them, but Af waited for me and fell into step beside me. As she chattered away, she didn’t seem to notice that I barely spoke a word as we made our way up the road to the local newsagents.

I bought my favourite chocolate Bounty and left the shop with a few of my friends. We stood outside and waited for Maria and Af to come out, but as we stood on the pavement, we noticed that a car full of boys had pulled up and parked a few yards away from the shop. As soon as Maria and Af emerged from the shop, the driver pulled down his car window and leaned out.

‘Oi you! Come over here, I wanna talk to you.’

The rest of us bristled, but without any hesitation, Af flicked her long dark hair over one of her shoulders and, hips swaying from side to side, she ambled across to the car.

I rolled my eyes and raised my eyebrows at Roshna as she blushed.

‘Come on guys, let’s go,’ said Roshna, sounding flustered as she started to walk back down the road, heading for school.

The rest of my friends followed her, but for some reason, I didn’t want to leave without Af. I looked over at her. She was leaning into the car, chatting and flicking her hair.

As I stood undecidedly, the driver stretched his neck out of the car. ‘Oi you! Come here, I wanna talk to you as well!’

I saw Af’s face fall and for a few seconds she appeared as though she was suddenly lost. Ever since I’d started at Alumwell Comprehensive, I’d seen cars full of boys cat-calling and whistling at girls, but I had never attracted their attention before. Now as I made my way to the car, I said a silent prayer that my parents would never find out that I had gone when I was called. I stopped right by the driver’s door.

His wide smirking mouth showed off his crooked, decaying brown teeth. ‘So, what’s your name then?’ he asked.

Ignoring him and his question, I took Af by her elbow and gave her a light tug away from the car. ‘Come on, let’s go. You’re not staying here.’

For a second, Af looked completely confused. Then without a word, she turned with me and we walked away from the car.

Despite my reservations and all of our differences, Af and I became inseparable after that day. But a few weeks later, at home my dad summoned me to him.

‘Jas, you’re friends with that Af girl? You know she’s a Muslim, don’t you?’

I frowned. How did my dad even know Af’s name? I’d never spoken about any of my school friends at home. Clearly, I was being spied on. I shouldn’t have been surprised. I didn’t know any Sikh Punjabi, Hindu or Muslim girls who hadn’t been spied on. It was considered normal for our parents to spy on us. A lot of parents openly boasted about watching their daughters when they were outside the home. I knew that some members of my own family sat outside their daughters’ schools and watched their daughters’ comings and goings. But until now I’d had no idea that my own parents were capable of doing the same.

‘Do you know what she is?’ asked my dad. ‘She’s not a good girl, ok? I’ve seen her myself talking to boys. You’re not to be friends with her anymore. Do you understand? There are plenty of other girls that you can be friends with. There must be lots of Sikh girls at your school that you can choose as friends who won’t lead you down the wrong path like this Muslim girl!’

I couldn’t tell my dad that there were lots of Sikh girls at my school who had walked ‘down the wrong path’ many times over and I knew that Af didn’t sleep around with guys, but even if she did, that was her business. And the fact that Af was a Muslim seemed to make her even less acceptable in my dad’s eyes. With growing anger, I listened silently to my dad as two thoughts swirled around inside my head. He’d spied on me and he was a hypocrite.

I already knew that my five sisters and I were closely watched inside and outside the house because we carried our family’s honour and reputation. And here’s the thing, my parents and the parents of other teenage girls made sure that their daughters knew that they were being spied on. Because if we knew that our parents were going to be watching them, we would monitor their own behaviour – it’s like living in a cage without any visible bars, but the bars are there. And so, it didn’t matter what we did or who we did it with, inside the house or outside at school, we always knew that we were likely to be watched.

Looking back, that was the cleverest part of it. Our parents didn’t have to watch us every minute of every day. They only had to make us believe that they might be watching us.

What floored me was how much my dad knew about Af before I’d even said a word about her at home. He’d told me her name, told me he’d seen her talking to boys with his own eyes and told me I wasn’t to be friends with her anymore. Which meant someone had been watching not just me, but spent time watching Af too.

How? When? What time?

Somehow, without me saying a word, information about my school life had reached him. I didn’t know whether I was being watched every day, occasionally, or whether relatives or family friends had simply reported what they saw.

It almost didn’t matter.

They didn’t need continuous surveillance for it to work – the possibility of surveillance was enough.

That’s why even though my dad hadn’t even been standing beside me when that car full of boys pulled up outside the newsagents, the moment one of those boys had called me over, my first thought had been: please don’t let my parents find out.

That was how deeply the spying had already worked its way into my everyday being.

What my dad was really protecting was the family’s honour and reputation – which lived, as it always did, inside his daughters’ behaviour and nowhere else.
