‘I got this.’ Kamal held up a cassette tape. ‘Have you got a cassette player?’ Dimple nodded. Despite not having any cassette tapes, Dimple had assumed sole ownership of an old cassette player that the old owner Guneet had left behind. ‘Will your mum shout at you if I play this at your house?’ asked Kamal. I frowned. I couldn’t understand why Kamal thought that my mother would shout at us for wanting to play a cassette tape.

Dimple laughed. ‘Don’t you have a cassette player of your own?’ ‘Yeah, but my grandfather doesn’t like it if I dance. He gets angry and shouts at me. I can teach you lot to dance if you like,’ she added. Dance? Both Dimple and I looked at each other. We’d never danced before.

‘You ask mum,’ I said to Dimple. She was my parents’ favourite and for some reason, it seemed that my mother went out of her way not to upset her. Dimple asked and my mother, looking bemused, said yes. With trepidation because we didn’t know whether Guneet’s old cassette player even worked, we plugged it into a socket in the living room and gingerly placed Kamal’s tape into the hatch. Dimple pressed play and we all listened entranced as the sound of Alaap blasted out of the small speakers.

At the sound of bhangra music, Sarb, Pardeep and Bina rushed across to join us and within a few minutes Kamal was teaching us all how to dance. Starting with basic bhangra moves, we all soon cottoned on. Eventually we managed to persuade my dad to buy us our own Alaap tape from the Indian video and music store down the road and over the rest of the summer holidays, we gradually built up our collection to include Golden Star, Apna Sangeet and DCS.
Our house became the only one in the row of houses that played music. Any time the other girls in the neighbourhood wanted to dance or heard our music, they would hurry over.

But not everyone liked it. One day, Sarb and our cousin and neighbour Manny were dancing in our garden when Dev, our next-door neighbour became angry with them. And he threw a steel pole at them. A grown man threw a steel pole at two young girls because they were dancing. It missed them by inches.

Even now, writing that sentence down makes the whole thing sound unbelievable. But it happened. Two girls were dancing in a garden in Walsall and an adult man became angry enough to throw a steel pole at them. By then, we were beginning to realise that Kamal’s grandfather wasn’t the only one who wanted to drain the fun out of living - there always seemed to be somebody who objected to girls having too much fun, being too loud, dancing too freely or simply behaving in a way that somebody else had decided girls shouldn’t behave.

Kamal’s grandfather had shouted at her for dancing. Dev had thrown a steel pole at Sarb and Manny for doing the same thing. And yet, inside our house, the music kept playing. My mother was part of a system that controlled us girls, but on that particular afternoon, she said yes. And that yes mattered. My parents let us dance. That might sound like a small thing. But for us it wasn’t. For us girls growing up in that environment, in those houses, under those expectations, being allowed to move your body to music was a kind of freedom that not everyone got.

We had rules - plenty of them - but the music stayed. By the time I was ten, Dimple and I were doing all of the chores in the house, washing up, cleaning and eventually doing the cooking as well. I can barely even remember a time when I wasn’t doing chores, but alongside all those rules and responsibilities, my parents allowed us to dance. That small piece of freedom mattered because we knew that not every girl even had that. There are so many things that I hate about the Indian culture, but there are also so many things I love… bhangra and Bollywood make all the rest of the nonsense just about bearable.

There is so much about the culture I grew up in that I have had to reckon with and if you’ve read my books, you’ll know that that reckoning hasn’t been simple or painless. But bhangra, Bollywood, the music that filled our house on that street – these things I have only ever loved. I’ll always remember the neighbourhood girls coming over to dance and the joy of music spreading into the lives of other girls because my mother had said yes to us dancing.
That was all it took for our house to feel freer than some of the others around us.

The joy of dancing has never left me. Anytime I feel depressed, I get up and dance. I don’t think about it. It just happens, the way it happened that first afternoon on the living room floor in Walsall when I was a girl who had never danced before and suddenly, I couldn't imagine a life without it. Some things survive everything. Dancing is one of mine.
